The robot, its systems, and how we decide

Luna Terps is a team of University of Maryland mechatronics engineering students working on NASA’s Lunabotics Challenge. This page explains the challenge, how the work is divided, and how decisions are recorded. Team-specific progress appears here only once it has been verified, and team members are named once they consent to publication.

Mission & competition

NASA’s Lunabotics Challenge asks college teams to design, build, and operate a prototype lunar construction robot. In each run the robot excavates regolith simulant, carries it across an obstacle zone, and deposits it to build a berm: the kind of structure that could protect future lunar sites from landing plume, heat, and radiation.

Luna Terps is working on a robot for this challenge. The engineering record on this site shows what the team is trying, how it decides, and who does the work.

Challenge facts

Arena
About 6.8 m × 5.0 m, filled with BP-1 crushed-basalt simulant about 45 cm deep
Obstacles
At least three boulders of about 30-40 cm and at least three craters
Berm target
Only berm volume inside a marked 2.2 m × 0.9 m area counts
Mission cycle
Excavate, cross loaded, deposit, cross back empty, repeat
Operation
Teleoperated or autonomous, from a mission control room outside the arena
Robot mass
80 kg maximum
Event
Construction runs at NASA’s Kennedy Space Center; the guidebook lists May 24-27, 2027, subject to change

Source: NASA Lunabotics Challenge Guidebook 2026-2027. Rules and dates change between seasons; check the current guidebook.

Systems that work together

Five areas share one robot and one schedule. A choice in one area becomes a constraint in the next, so each area is described by what it owns and what it shapes.

  • Mechanical

    Owns
    Chassis, drivetrain, excavation, and deposition hardware that must keep working in fine, abrasive simulant.
    Shapes
    Sets the mass budget, envelope, and mounting points every other system builds on.

  • Electrical

    Owns
    Batteries, power distribution, motor control, wiring, and the emergency stop.
    Shapes
    Powers each mechanism and gives software dependable sensor and motor signals.

  • Autonomy / software

    Owns
    Perception, localization, path planning, and control of dig-and-deposit cycles, plus the operator tools.
    Shapes
    Depends on sensor placement and motor behavior; decides what operators need to see.

  • Science / operations

    Owns
    How regolith behaves, run planning, test procedures, safety, and mission-control roles.
    Shapes
    Turns requirements into test plans and checks each system against them.

  • Business / outreach

    Owns
    Documentation, recruiting, sponsor relations, and this site.
    Shapes
    Makes sure every kind of work on the robot is explained clearly to readers and supporters.

How we make decisions

Every recorded decision follows the same path from question to result, so a reader can check the reasoning and not only the outcome.

  1. Set the context

    State the goal, the constraint, and what is already known.

  2. Name the challenge

    Write down the specific engineering question or uncertainty.

  3. Compare options

    Lay out practical alternatives and the evidence that can be shared for each.

  4. Decide and explain

    Record the choice and the reasons, including what would change it.

  5. Report the result

    Describe what was observed without overstating it, then name the next check.
